# Persona Flow Review Methodology

## Purpose

The review connects four evidence layers:

```text
Persona evidence
→ Agreed story scope and business objective (ClickUp, local story, PRD)
→ Figma design decision
→ Explained status, issue, or talking point
```

It answers three questions for each story:

1. Does the design support the user's goal and the intended journey?
2. Does it fulfil the agreed scope and support the business objective?
3. Can the designer explain to the client why it is a good solution?

The AI is an evidence-constrained reviewer. It is not a synthetic user and does not replace usability testing. Accessibility, visual-design, and design-system rules are secondary. They matter when they change the user's outcome or are explicit requirements.

## Evidence rules

### Valid persona evidence

- Research-backed goals and desired outcomes
- Responsibilities and task ownership
- Information needed for decisions
- Documented concerns and failure conditions
- Relevant working behavior
- Contextual constraints

### Do not treat as independent persona evidence

- Proposed screens or controls
- “The product should…” statements
- Product promises written into the persona
- Decorative biography
- Demographics with no effect on the task
- Assumptions generated by the reviewer

### Valid story evidence

- User-facing acceptance criteria
- Preconditions and triggers
- Required information and actions
- Required feedback and states
- Success conditions
- Explicit exclusions
- Approved decisions and amendments
- ClickUp task descriptions
- ClickUp comments that add, remove, change, or clarify behavior, with their author and date
- Local story requirements and PRD rules that ClickUp does not contradict

Technical criteria are relevant only when they affect visible behavior, timing, permission, data safety, or system feedback.

### Not story decisions

- Unanswered questions in ClickUp comments
- Status changes, assignments, and reactions
- Comments about implementation only, with no visible effect
- PRD statements that a later ClickUp decision explicitly replaced

### Valid business evidence

- Business goals or objectives in the PRD or product brief
- The business reason in a ClickUp task or comment
- The "so that" line of a user story, when it names a business outcome
- Documented business processes, rules, and operational needs, such as an approval step or a legal obligation

### Not business evidence

- Generic benefits such as higher conversion, lower cost, or better retention, when no source states them as a goal
- Industry statistics or best-practice claims
- Benefits the reviewer assumes

Phrase every business benefit as an expected outcome, such as "should help" or "is designed to". Never present it as a proven result.

## Source authority

| Source | Role |
| --- | --- |
| ClickUp task and comments | Agreed scope of each story. The latest explicit decision wins. |
| Local story files and decision records | Context, dependencies, references, and requirements ClickUp does not contradict |
| PRD and product documents, including UX or experience specs | Product intent and business rules where ClickUp is silent |
| Persona documents | Target users' goals, needs, constraints, and behavior |
| Figma page | The design under review |

Authority never hides a conflict. For each difference between sources:

- **An explicit ClickUp decision covers it:** follow ClickUp, and record what it overrides.
- **ClickUp is silent:** use the local story or PRD requirement, labelled with its source.
- **No explicit decision covers it, and it would change a verdict:** ask the designer, or list it as an open scope question.

## Source summary

Every review keeps a list of sources with their status:

- **Read:** the contents were opened and inspected.
- **Partly read:** some contents could not be reached, such as attachments or comment pages.
- **Not accessible:** the connector or file could not be reached.
- **Not found:** discovery found no such source, such as a missing PRD.

A source counts as reviewed only when its status is Read or Partly read.

## Persona selection

Use the explicit story actor whenever available.

If it is missing, use:

1. Scenario mapping
2. Role and permissions
3. Task ownership
4. Persona goals

Use multiple personas only when each has a distinct role in the flow. Review their perspectives separately.

## Figma interpretation

- Match by story ID before title.
- Treat each top-level story section as one review unit.
- Record section and screen node IDs.
- Use connector or prototype relationships for order.
- Treat nested sections as states or branches only when their naming and connections support that interpretation.
- Do not infer interaction from appearance alone.
- Do not assume a component instance has the same content as its source component; inspect the visible instance.
- When a story's flow is missing from the page, or a required state is missing, that is a design gap. Use Insufficient evidence only when the page, the flow, or the story's sources could not be reached.

## Review questions

### Coverage

- Can the persona reach the stated outcome?
- Is every important requirement represented?
- Are critical non-happy states represented?
- Does the flow include excluded behavior?
- Does information that drives a decision stay consistent from one step to the next?

### Step-level walkthrough

- Does the user have a reason to take the intended action?
- Is that action visible?
- Does its label connect to the desired result?
- Does the next state show progress or success?

These questions identify likely friction. They do not predict actual behavior.

### Business fit

- Which documented business objective does the story serve?
- Does the design support that objective and the process around it?
- Can the design be explained in terms of user value and business relevance?

## What to flag

### The decision rule

Ask before recording any issue:

> Does this represent a meaningful, evidence-backed gap between the agreed requirements and the design, or does it materially prevent the intended user from achieving their goal?

If the answer is no, do not create an issue. It does not appear in the canvas, gets no fix, and does not affect the status.

### Not design issues

| Looks like | Why it is not flagged |
| --- | --- |
| Example figures that do not add up, such as a total lower than one of its parts | Mock data. Developers build from the rule, not the sample numbers. |
| English copy on a product that ships in another language | Figma designs are made in English. Developers handle localization. |
| Missing translations or language variants | An implementation task, unless the scope makes it a design deliverable |
| Typos and small wording differences | Copy polish, not a flow or requirement gap |
| Cosmetic details and stylistic preferences | No effect on the user's task or an agreed requirement |
| Edge cases with no evidence they matter | Not part of the agreed scope |
| Generic design advice | Applying a convention is not evidence of a user problem |
| Accessibility, visual, or design-system rule breaks with no real user impact | Secondary unless explicitly in scope |

### Always flag

A copy, number, or localization problem is a real issue when it:

- Changes the user's decision, such as a wrong price or a wrong count of people to be contacted.
- Causes a wrong transaction or record.
- Contradicts a documented business rule.
- Breaks a required flow.
- Falls under localization work the agreed scope says must be designed in Figma, such as language-specific layouts or locale-dependent content.

### When both lists match

For number problems, ask whether the user acts on that number:

- The user reads it to follow progress, such as a scorecard, target, or board. Do not flag wrong example figures, even when they change a percentage, color, or status.
- The user approves, sends, pays, or deletes based on it, such as an approval count or an invoice total. Flag it.
- Two screens state different rules, such as "stops after 3 follow-ups" next to a timeline with 5. Flag it.

## Finding boundaries

### User need

An evidence-backed problem or outcome that is independent of a particular interface.

### Story requirement

Agreed product behavior or a condition the story must satisfy.

### Design decision

A chosen screen, sequence, control, label, state, or presentation.

### UX friction

An evidence-backed risk that makes the agreed task harder to understand or complete.

### Requirement gap

Required behavior is absent or contradicted.

### Scope conflict

The design contradicts an explicit exclusion or a business rule, or changes agreed behavior. An addition that no source asks for, but that breaks nothing, is not a scope conflict. It is either worth keeping (see [Beyond-scope elements](#beyond-scope-elements)) or ignored.

### Mapping drift

Figma and source documents refer to different stories, IDs, or current behavior.

### Accessibility concern

A barrier that materially affects the user's outcome, or breaks an explicit accessibility requirement. Route it to an accessibility or design-audit workflow. It never sets a story's status on its own. Do not claim conformance here.

### Evidence gap

The available material does not support a reliable conclusion.

### Source conflict

ClickUp, the local story, and the PRD disagree, and no explicit decision resolves it. This is a question for the product owner, not a design issue. List it under "Open scope questions" with what each source says.

## Status rubric

Every story gets a status reason: one or two sentences that name the design behavior behind the status. Never repeat the story or just say it meets requirements.

A **core requirement** is one that the story's title, main goal, or success state names. Other acceptance criteria and agreed states are **important requirements**. Only confirmed issues set the status. A potential concern is shown for the designer to check, but never lowers the status on its own.

### Supported

Use when:

- The user can reach the goal through a complete path.
- Every core requirement is met.
- No core contradiction blocks the story.

Small issues may remain. Explain:

- **Why it is supported:** the specific screens or behavior that meet the requirements.
- **User value:** how it helps the user reach the goal.
- **Business value:** why that matters to the documented business objective. When none is documented, say so.
- **Talking point:** a sentence the designer can say in the walkthrough.

### Partially supported

Use when:

- The user can reach the goal.
- An important requirement, state, or connection is missing or incomplete.

Explain:

- **What works:** up to three supported parts, each tied to a screen.
- **What is missing:** each gap as an issue, with its user impact, business impact when relevant, and the smallest fix.
- **Talking point:** what the design achieves today, without presenting the gap as done.

Keep confirmed gaps apart from potential concerns, using the confidence label.

### Needs attention

Use when:

- A core requirement is missing or contradicted.
- The story's main goal cannot be reached reliably, or its flow is missing.
- A safety, trust, or scope problem changes the intended solution.

A design detail that is not ideal is never enough. Explain:

- **Why it needs attention:** the specific mismatch, omission, contradiction, or missing flow.
- **Each issue:** the affected requirement, user impact, business impact, and recommended fix.
- **Talking point:** what is safe to tell the client before the fix, when anything is.

### Insufficient evidence

Use only when:

- The Figma page or the story's flow could not be reached, and no export was supplied.
- The story's scope sources could not be reached.

Explain what could not be reached and what is needed to finish the review. Do not use this status for design gaps.

### Disputed requirements

When sources dispute a core requirement and the designer could not settle it, assess the story against the undisputed requirements. Say in the status reason that one requirement waits on a decision. List the dispute under "Open scope questions".

## Priority and compression

Rank findings by:

1. Objective blocked
2. Core requirement or scope conflict
3. Safety or trust risk
4. Meaningful task friction
5. Clarification

Keep a maximum of three issues per story. Do not create issues to fill the quota. Merge duplicates, and report a problem that spans stories once, as a project-wide issue.

Priority and confidence labels are defined in `SKILL.md` section 7. For a potential concern, say what would confirm it.

Each issue contains:

1. A short, plain-language title that states the problem
2. Priority and confidence labels
3. **Affected requirement:** the criterion, decision, or business rule, in one plain sentence
4. **What we found:** what is visible or missing, described by name
5. **User impact:** what the user cannot do, may misunderstand, or could get wrong
6. **Business impact:** the consequence for the business objective or process, when there is one
7. **Recommended fix:** the smallest meaningful change
8. A screen name in words, linked to the exact Figma node
9. **Evidence:** the sources it relies on, as named links, such as "ClickUp comment, 12 Oct 2026" or "Story file"

Write every part in plain English (see `SKILL.md` section 9). Keep requirement IDs, node IDs, and layer names in links, not in sentences.

## Design rationale

Besides issues, the review finds design decisions worth defending. They reach the client in four forms, all built from the same evidence:

| Output | Where it lives | What it is for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Figma comments | Beside the feature in Figma | The client's team reviews the design without the designer, and can explain it internally |
| Beyond-scope annotations | On the screen, in Figma Dev Mode | The client confirms an addition no story asks for |
| Talking points | The canvas's Client meeting view | The designer presents each story |
| Walkthrough script | The Client meeting view, on request | The designer presents the whole journey |

None of them is an issue, and none changes a status. Issues say what is missing. Rationale says why something works, and never turns an assumption into a strength.

### Which decisions deserve a comment

Take candidates from Supported and Partially supported stories: the decisions behind "What works", the status reason, the value statements, and scope decisions. A candidate becomes a comment only when it passes all five tests:

1. **It is a decision.** The designer chose it, and it could reasonably have been different: the order of steps, a default, what is shown or hidden, a limit, a state, or a label that sets an expectation. A required element that is simply present is not a decision, and neither is visual polish.
2. **A reason backs it.** At least one of these, strongest first:
   - A user need, goal, or pain point that a persona or story documents.
   - A business objective or rule that a source documents.
   - A design principle applied to this user and screen, in plain words, with its effect here. "The excluded people are listed before any message exists, so Riley fixes a wrong list while it still costs nothing" counts. "Progressive disclosure" or "best practice" on its own does not.
   - What follows directly from how the design works, such as "the month total is the sum of the days, so the two always agree".

   Domain logic the client's team would accept, such as what an ended placement means, also counts, but a comment that rests on it sets `checkFirst`.
3. **It is not obvious.** A reviewer could question it, change it, or miss its point. Skip what the screen or its labels already explain, and what most products do, such as a search box on a long list.
4. **It is safe to praise.** No open issue names its screen, including a potential concern, and the comment does not praise the behavior an issue is about. Other screens of the same story can still get one.
5. **It is not a repeat.** No other comment or annotation gives the same reason.

Drop generic praise ("clean", "intuitive", "user-friendly", "modern"), descriptions of the screen that give no reason, and claims that no source or logic supports.

### Rank and limit

List the strongest candidate from every eligible story before choosing. Never stop after the first few good ones. Rank everything that passed, across the whole review, by:

1. **Impact:** it serves the user's main goal or a documented business objective, not a side detail.
2. **Challenge risk:** the client's team is likely to question it, change it, or misread it. Typical examples are a default, a trade-off, a restriction on who can do what, a rule that works differently from what a reviewer expects, and a state that prevents a wrong reading, such as missing data shown as a message instead of a zero.
3. **Evidence:** a documented need or objective beats a design principle, and a principle beats what follows from the design alone.

| Limit | Ceiling |
| --- | --- |
| Recommended comments per review, counting every reviewed story | 5 for eight stories or fewer, 6 for 9 to 11, 7 for 12 to 14, 8 for 15 or more |
| Optional comments | Up to the same number, from candidates that passed every test but ranked below the cut |
| Per screen | 1. A second only for a separate feature with a different benefit. Optional comments count. |
| Per comment | 25 to 45 words, two or three short sentences, one point. Count words by spaces. |

These are ceilings, not quotas. Comments already in Figma count toward them. Expect about one recommended comment for every two or three stories with a clear strength. There is no minimum. When nothing passes, suggest nothing, and the canvas says so.

Optional comments have `additional: true` and a `question`. They wait, collapsed, until the designer adds them.

### Connect the decision to value

Write each comment as a short chain, in natural sentences:

1. **The decision**, named by what the client sees.
2. **What it does for the user**, tied to their need.
3. **What it does for the business**, only when a source documents the objective. Otherwise stop at the user. Never mention that the objective is missing, and never force a business reason.
4. **What it protects or prevents**, when the logic supports it.

Phrase unverified value as intent: "is designed to", "should help". Never invent numbers or outcomes, and never present a hope as a result.

Record the reasoning in the comment's fields, so the designer sees it beside the wording:

- `userNeed`: the documented need.
- `businessGoal`: the documented objective, only when a source states it.
- `principle`: the design principle, in plain words, with its effect here, when it is part of the reason.
- `question`: what the client's team is likely to ask, in their words and from what they see, such as "Why are targets set per day and not per week?". Required for optional comments, and useful on any comment. It names no internal source, and it is not part of the comment.
- `checkFirst`: the assumption the designer should confirm before publishing, such as "This assumes an ended placement means the candidate stopped working for the client." The canvas shows it as a warning, and Approve all skips the comment, so the designer approves it on its own.
- `alsoOn`: other screens where the same reasoning applies, when one comment covers them all.
- `why`: for the designer, why this decision was picked over the others.

### Reasons the client can use

Comments, annotations, talking points, and the script are read or heard by the client. Each one explains a decision so the client, and the designer, can defend it with logic from the user's and the business's point of view.

**Keep internal sources out.** Never mention ClickUp, the story or its comments, the product owner, the PRD, internal discussions, or phrases such as "as agreed", "as requested", or "the story says". These sources prove the decision to the designer, so they stay in the evidence links and the `why` field.

**Give the logic instead.** Use one or more of these, in this order:

1. The user need or business goal a source documents, in plain words.
2. What follows directly from how the design works, such as "the month total is the sum of the days, so the two always agree".
3. Basic domain logic the client's own team would accept as true for their business, such as "an ended placement is a candidate who stopped working for a client". Never invent numbers, outcomes, or business practices. When a note relies on this, set `checkFirst`, so the designer confirms it before publishing.

**Then say what it protects or prevents:** what would go wrong for the user or the business if the decision changed, when the logic supports it.

A scope decision is explained the same way. The note says why the design works this way, not that it differs from the story or who agreed to it.

A beyond-scope annotation, or a script line that asks the client to confirm an addition, may say it goes beyond the agreed scope, because that is the client's decision. It still gives the user reason, and names no internal source.

**Test:** if the client forwarded the note to a colleague who has never seen the project's tickets, would it still make sense and convince them?

For daily targets that replaced weekly ones:

- Avoid: "Targets are entered per day, as agreed in the story comments."
- Good: "Targets are entered per person, per day. The week, month and quarter targets are the sum of those days, so all four periods always agree, and a holiday or a new joiner only changes the days it affects."

### Edge cases

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| The user need and the business need pull apart, such as an approval step that slows the user down | Comment only when the design settles it in a way a source supports, and name both sides plainly: "It adds one check before sending, so nothing leaves without a sign-off." When nothing settles it, it is an open scope question, not a comment. |
| No business objective is documented | Give the user reason only. |
| The only support is a design principle or domain logic | Allowed when the principle is applied to this screen. Rank it below documented reasons. Set `checkFirst` when it rests on domain logic. |
| No support at all | Drop it, however good it looks. |
| The same reasoning appears on several screens, such as one filter pattern on three lists | One comment, on the screen where it matters most. List the others in `alsoOn`. Never post the same reason twice. |
| The story is incomplete, such as missing acceptance criteria or a state it does not define | Comment only on what the story or persona documents. When the design fills a gap the story leaves open, never present that choice as agreed. A new element is beyond scope. An interpretation that changes the outcome is an open scope question. |
| The story is Partially supported | Comment only on what works today, on screens no open issue names. |
| A scope decision changed the original story | A strong candidate, because reviewers expect the original. Explain why it works this way, never where it was decided. |
| The design breaks a common pattern on purpose | A strong candidate. Explain the reason for this user. |
| The comment is already in Figma from an earlier run | Keep it and its ID, as `SKILL.md` section 10 describes. |

### Writing a comment

- Names the actual feature, section, or interaction.
- Explains any product term in the comment itself, because each comment is read on its own.
- Uses simple, natural English a non-technical client understands.
- Never sounds defensive, and never guesses at a reason.
- Never mentions implementation details, visual polish, or metrics no source states.

Pin it to the most specific node that shows the feature, such as a card or panel. Use the screen when no smaller node fits. Figma does not accept comments on a layer inside a component instance, so Publish mode pins those to the nearest layer it accepts, at the feature's position.

### Beyond-scope elements

Some designs include elements no source asks for that are worth keeping. Keep one only when all of these are true:

1. No ClickUp task, story, or PRD section asks for it.
2. It does not contradict an exclusion or business rule, and does not change agreed behavior. Otherwise it is a scope conflict issue.
3. Its value ties to the persona or a documented business goal.

Ignore additions with no evidence of value. Do not annotate, list, or flag them, and leave no trace of them in the canvas.

Keep up to 5 per review, one per feature. For each, record what it is, why it is beyond scope, the user value, the business value when a source supports it, a short note for developers when useful, and the annotation text.

**Annotation text:** starts with "Beyond the story:", stays at 40 words or fewer, says what the element does and why it helps, and asks the client to confirm it stays. It goes in a Figma annotation in the "Beyond scope" category, never in a comment.

A feature gets either a comment or an annotation, never both. A separate feature on the same screen can get an annotation while another gets a comment. Annotations do not count toward the per-screen comment limit.

### Talking points

Every story except Insufficient evidence gets one: one or two sentences, at most about 40 words, that the designer can say aloud. Build it from this chain, then write it naturally:

```text
The user's goal or problem
→ How the design addresses it
→ Why that fits the user's context
→ The business objective it supports, when a source documents one
```

> Before Riley sets up any messages, the check shows exactly who is excluded and why. That gives Riley one clear moment to confirm the list is safe, which is designed to protect opt-outs and customer relationships.

- Use this project's people, screens, and goals. Never reuse a talking point across stories.
- For Partially supported and Needs attention stories, cover only what works today. Never present a missing capability as done.
- Avoid empty claims such as "This improves UX" or "This follows best practices".

### Walkthrough script

One script for the whole review, written to be spoken.

- **When:** only when the designer asks, with the Generate button in the canvas or in a chat. A review run never writes or rewrites it.
- **References:** each stage lists every story it presents in `storyIds`. The canvas shows the story IDs and a link to each story's flow in Figma, and copies them with the script.
- **Order:** follow the user's journey, not the status order. One stage per journey step or key screen.
- **Length:** two to four sentences per stage, about 50 to 100 words. A typical review comes to 300 to 500 words. A small review is shorter. Never pad it.
- **Each stage:** what the user is trying to do, how the design helps, why the key decision fits this user when a persona source supports it, and the business objective it supports when a source documents one. Skip a part that no source supports.
- **Gaps:** one honest sentence for each open issue that affects the journey, with the fix or next step, on top of the stage's sentences. Never hide a gap behind positive words, and never present it as done.
- **Beyond scope:** one sentence per element, asking the client to confirm it, on top of the stage's sentences.
- **Insufficient evidence:** one neutral closing sentence, such as "We'll walk through approval once we can review it." It is not a stage, and it never suggests the design is unfinished.
- **Reasons:** explain each decision by its user and business reasons, never by an internal source.
- **Style:** conversational, short sentences, practical value. No introduction or closing, no reading out every element, no repeating the stories, no sales tone, no invented outcomes.

Reuse the talking points as raw material, but do not paste them in word for word.

### Quality check

Before showing the rationale, check every comment against the five tests and the limits above, and every comment, annotation, talking point, and script stage against these:

- **Reasons, not sources:** it gives user and business reasons, and names no internal source or who decided.
- **Honest:** no hope is stated as a result, and every assumption behind a comment is in its `checkFirst`.
- **Clear:** a client who has not read the stories understands it.
- **Still true:** a note already in Figma still describes the current screen. When the design changed, it gets a `reviewNote`, as `SKILL.md` section 10 describes.
- **Not repeated:** no two notes make the same point, and no feature gets a second note when it already has one in Figma. The script may cover the same decisions in its own words, never word for word.

Drop any note that fails. Fewer strong notes beat many weak ones.

## Fixing issues

A review finds issues. Fixing them is a separate, designer-driven step, one issue at a time.

### Which issues can be fixed

| Classification | Fix action | What a fix does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Requirement gap | `figma-screen` or `figma-new-screen` | Adds or corrects the missing behavior, state, or screen |
| UX friction | `figma-screen` | Clarifies the label, order, emphasis, or feedback |
| Scope conflict | `figma-screen` | Removes or changes the conflicting behavior. The note card says this may be a product decision. |
| Mapping drift | `figma-rename` | Renames the Figma section to match the current story. Story files are never edited. |
| Accessibility concern | `audit-handoff` | Starts a separate accessibility review |
| Evidence gap or source conflict | `none` | Nothing to fix in Figma. A source conflict needs a scope decision first. |

Strengths and anything that failed the "worth flagging" test are not issues, so they never get a fix.

Use `figma-new-screen` only when the missing behavior needs its own screen or state, such as a missing error state.

### Principles

- **One issue at a time.** Each fix has its own chat, its own copy in Figma, and its own approval.
- **Safe copy first.** A fix is made on detached copies on the "Fixed issues" page. The Wireflows and Draft pages do not change until the designer approves.
- **The designer decides.** A designer instruction overrides the suggested fix. Conflicts with the story or scope are recorded, not blocked.
- **The source is updated, not the copy.** An approved fix is applied to the screen's main component on the Draft page. The Wireflows page then updates through its instances.
- **The assessment is earned.** After a fix is applied, the story is re-reviewed. Its assessment changes only when the evidence supports it.
- **Shared screens are flagged.** When the same component appears in other stories, those stories are marked for re-review, not silently re-assessed.

## Guardrails

These hold in every mode:

- **Evidence:** no first-person persona role-play, invented feelings or behavior, or design preference without evidence. No business benefit without a source, and none presented as a proven result. No claim that ClickUp, Figma, or a file was reviewed unless its contents were read.
- **Scope:** no scope claim without an approved scope source, no silent choice between conflicting sources, no generic persona in place of a defined one, and no PRD content invented when the PRD cannot be found.
- **Findings:** no issue that fails the "worth flagging" rule, including English copy, example-figure arithmetic, and typos that meet no "Always flag" condition. No Insufficient evidence status for a design gap, no project-wide finding repeated under every story, no flow order taken from canvas coordinates alone, and no WCAG compliance claim.
- **Figma:** only the page the designer named is reviewed. Review mode changes nothing. Fix mode changes only the "Fixed issues" page. The Draft and Wireflows pages change only after the designer approves.
- **Rationale:** no note that hides a gap or turns an assumption into a strength, and no talking point reused across stories or presenting a gap as done. Nothing is published or annotated without the designer's approval, and nothing is shown as published unless the Figma API confirmed it.
- **Secrets:** no personal access token in any file, canvas, comment, script, log, or error message.
- **Writing:** no jargon, codes, or shorthand that forces the reader to look something up.
